# Fractional Real Estate for Retirees: What the Income Covers and What the Lockup Costs

**Dek:** A clear-eyed look at what fractional real estate income actually covers in retirement, why the 5 to 10 year lockup weighs heavier after 65, and how K-1 timing can upend a filing rhythm built on 1099s.

## Quick Answer (60 seconds)

Fractional real estate can supplement retirement income, but it should never anchor it. Platform-reported yields typically run 4 to 9% annually, paid from rental cash flow, and PSFnetwork lets you start at $100. ## What income can retirees actually expect from fractional real estate?

Platform-reported annual yields in fractional real estate typically run 4 to 9%, paid from rental cash flow after fees. On a $10,000 position, a 7% yield means about $700 a year before taxes. That is real money, but it is a supplement, not a pension. Distributions are variable, tied to occupancy and expenses, and no return is promised to anyone.

Whether you reinvest changes the arithmetic. Ten thousand dollars at a 7% yield with distributions reinvested annually grows to about $19,672 in 10 years. Taking distributions as cash instead produces $17,000 in total: the $10,000 principal plus $700 a year for 10 years. Reinvesting adds about $2,672. Most retirees take the cash, and that is a reasonable choice, but make it knowingly. The [compound interest calculator](https://www.investor.gov/financial-tools-calculators/calculators/compound-interest-calculator) at Investor.gov lets you test your own assumptions, and our guide to [building passive income with real estate](/blog/how-to-build-passive-income-with-real-estate) walks through payout mechanics in more depth.

Fees deserve equal attention. A 1% annual management fee held across a 10 year term quietly consumes roughly a tenth of your starting capital, and that is before upfront and exit fees. On a 4% yield, that bite is severe. On a 9% yield, it stings less. Either way, for a retiree the fee schedule is not fine print. It is the income statement.

## Why does the lockup matter more after 65?

Because plans change faster than properties sell. Typical fractional holds run 5 to 10 years, and secondary markets, where they exist at all, are thin. A 66 year old committing to a 10 year hold is betting that health, housing, and family needs stay stable until age 76. Money you might need inside that window does not belong in an illiquid position.

The industry pitches income products to retirees on the yield number, and we think that is the wrong pitch. For a retiree the binding constraint is almost never yield. It is the calendar: a 5 to 10 year lockup set against a time horizon where plans change fast, and a K-1 that can arrive in September set against a filing rhythm built on 1099s. A brochure can make the percentage look like the headline. The lockup is the headline.

Our own position is blunt. We would rather lose a retiree's investment than their liquidity. If the money might be needed for health or family inside five years, we think it does not belong on our platform or any platform like ours. Someone in Paul's position should keep true reserves in insured deposits and short-term instruments, and commit only what his plan can genuinely strand for a decade.

## How does K-1 timing hit a retiree's tax filing?

Fractional positions structured as pass-through LLCs issue a Schedule K-1, not the 1099 most retirees are used to. The partnership return deadline for tax year 2025 is March 16, 2026, and a Form 7004 extension can push your K-1's arrival to September 15. That timing can force you to extend your own return, a rhythm change many retirees find genuinely disruptive.

The content of the form surprises people too. Depreciation flows through the LLC, which can make the taxable income on your K-1 smaller than the cash you actually received, subject to the passive activity rules. That is often favorable. It is never simple. And one property can mean multiple state filings, because the state where the building sits may want its own return regardless of where you live. The [IRS instructions for Schedule K-1](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1065sk1) are the primary source here, and our guide to [how fractional real estate is taxed](/blog/how-fractional-real-estate-is-taxed) translates them into plain English.

Some pass-through income may also qualify for the [Section 199A qualified business income deduction](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/qualified-business-income-deduction) of up to 20%, which can soften the ordinary income treatment. May qualify is the operative phrase. Whether yours does depends on the offering's structure and your overall return, which is exactly the kind of question worth putting to a tax professional before you invest, not after the K-1 arrives.

## Are REITs a better fit for retirees who need daily liquidity?

For retirees who need daily liquidity, often yes. Publicly traded REITs sell in seconds on an exchange, must distribute at least 90% of taxable income, and report on standard tax forms with familiar timing. You give up direct property-level ownership and the per-square-foot framing, but you keep the exit. For income you may need to touch, tradability usually beats structure.

The tax treatment differs as well. REIT dividends are generally taxed as ordinary income, and some REIT dividends may also qualify for the Section 199A deduction. You will typically receive a 1099 on the familiar winter schedule rather than a K-1 that may show up after your accountant has left for the summer. For a retiree who values a predictable filing season, that alone is a real argument. The SEC's overview of [real estate investment trusts](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/real-estate-investment-trusts-reits) at Investor.gov is a good neutral starting point.

We are not neutral here, and we will not pretend to be. Fractional ownership gives you a [claim on a specific property](/blog/square-foot-real-estate-ownership-explained) rather than a traded pool, and for money you can truly commit for 5 to 10 years, we believe that specificity has value. But for the income slice of a retirement budget, the slice that pays bills, the ability to sell today usually matters more than what you own. Match the instrument to the job.

## Can you hold fractional real estate inside an IRA?

Generally only through a self-directed IRA with a specialty custodian, and the mechanics deserve respect. Leveraged property inside an IRA can trigger unrelated business income tax through the debt-financed income rules, and prohibited transaction rules bar any self-dealing with disqualified persons. For most retirees drawing down accounts rather than contributing, the added custodian cost and paperwork rarely earn their keep.

The rules exist because Congress fenced retirement accounts off from self-dealing. Renting the IRA-owned property to your daughter is out. So is using it yourself. The IRS pages on [unrelated business income tax](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/unrelated-business-income-tax) explain how debt-financed income creates UBIT even inside a tax-advantaged account, and the custodian will charge for the compliance work either way.

Context on limits helps frame the decision. For 2025, the employee 401(k) deferral limit is $23,500 and the IRA contribution limit is $7,000. If you are still contributing at those levels while semi-retired, conventional assets inside conventional accounts remain the simpler path, and a small taxable fractional position, sized to money you will not need, keeps the paperwork where you can see it.

## What this does not solve

Fractional real estate is not a bond substitute, an emergency fund, or a pension. A bond pays a contractual coupon and returns principal on a defined maturity date, and Treasuries carry the backing of the US government. Fractional distributions are variable, have no maturity date, and are not insured by any government agency. That is the honest baseline, and no marketing changes it.

Nor is it a bank account. [FDIC insurance](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/) covers bank deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. None of that protection applies here. If you are 65 or older and still interested after all of the above, our sizing discipline looks like this:

1. **Cover the floor first.** Essential monthly expenses should be met by Social Security, pension income, and insured deposits before any illiquid dollar is committed.
2. **Cap the position.** Size it so that losing every dollar would not change your housing, health care, or daily life.
3. **Check the calendar, not just the yield.** Assume the full 5 to 10 year hold, and assume the secondary market will not be there when you want it.
4. **Read the filings.** A Regulation A issuer files a public annual report, Form 1-K, which you can pull up with [EDGAR full-text search](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/) before you commit anything.
5. **Brief your tax preparer.** Tell them a K-1 is coming, that it may arrive as late as September 15, and ask whether extending your return changes anything else in your plan.

Start smaller than feels necessary. You can always add later.

## FAQ

**Q: Is fractional real estate a safe income source for retirees?**
A: No investment offering 4 to 9% variable yields is safe in the way insured deposits are safe. Distributions depend on rents and expenses, principal is at risk, and no return is promised. It can be a reasonable supplement for money you will not need for 5 to 10 years, but it should not carry essential expenses.

**Q: How much income would a $10,000 position produce?**
A: At a 7% platform-reported yield, about $700 a year taken as cash, which totals $17,000 over a decade including your principal. Reinvesting every distribution instead grows the position to about $19,672 in 10 years, roughly $2,672 more. Yields vary between 4 and 9% and are not fixed.

**Q: Can I sell my position if I suddenly need money for medical bills?**
A: You should assume no. Secondary markets for fractional positions are thin where they exist at all, and typical holds run 5 to 10 years. Money that might be needed for health or family belongs in liquid, insured accounts instead.

**Q: Will a fractional investment complicate my tax filing?**
A: It adds a Schedule K-1, which can arrive as late as September 15 if the issuer extends, and one property can require filings in more than one state. Depreciation may make your taxable income smaller than the cash you received, limited by passive activity rules. Many retirees hire a preparer for the first year.

**Q: Do I need to be an accredited investor?**
A: Not for Regulation A Tier 2 offerings, which can raise up to $75 million per 12 months and are open to non-accredited investors, capped at 10% of the greater of annual income or net worth per offering. Accredited status, meaning $1 million net worth excluding your primary residence or $200,000 income ($300,000 joint), opens Regulation D deals as well.

**Q: Is fractional real estate FDIC insured?**
A: No. FDIC insurance covers bank deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category, and a fractional real estate position is not a bank deposit. It is not insured by any other government agency either.
