# Questions to Ask Fractional Real Estate Platforms Before You Invest

**Dek:** Ten written questions, grouped into four themes, and one rule: if an answer cannot be verified in a filing, it does not count.

[VISUAL-HERO-01]

---

## Quick Answer (60 seconds)

Before you fund an account on any fractional real estate platform, get written answers to ten questions across four themes: filings and structure (which entity issues your shares, under which securities framework, and where the filings live on EDGAR), custody (who holds your money in escrow and who keeps the official share register), economics (all three fee layers and whether advertised numbers are gross or net), and the endgame (completed exits, backup servicing, and redemption terms). Every answer should be verifiable in the offering circular or on EDGAR, not just stated on a marketing page. If a platform cannot answer in writing, do not wire the money.

**Stat cards:**
- **10** - written questions to ask before funding an account
- **$75 million** - annual ceiling for a Regulation A Tier 2 offering
- **1/10** - of starting capital roughly consumed by a 1% annual fee over a 10-year hold
- **5 to 10 years** - typical hold period for fractional real estate deals

*Fractional real estate is illiquid, distributions are variable, and no return is promised.*

## Opening

Hannah, a pharmacist in Minneapolis, sits at her kitchen counter drafting an email to a fractional platform's support inbox. Her account is created, her bank is linked, and the funding button is one click away. Instead of clicking it, she is writing questions: who actually issues these shares, who holds my money before closing, what do the fees total across a full hold? The email is short. Sending it first is the whole strategy.

That email is the cheapest due diligence available to an individual investor, and almost nobody sends it. This guide gives you the ten questions worth putting in it, grouped into four themes, with a plain explanation of why each matters and what a good written answer contains. None of the questions require a finance degree. All of them produce answers you can check against public filings.

## Why shouldn't returns be your first question?

Most due-diligence lists tell you to ask about returns first. Returns are the one answer a platform cannot honestly give you; the ten questions worth asking are all about structure, and none of them mention yield. A projection is a marketing artifact. Structure is a fact you can verify. Ask who issues your shares, who holds your money, what the fees total, and how the deal ends.

Put all ten in a single email before you fund anything, and ask for replies in writing. We think the quality of a platform's answers matters as much as the answers themselves; a support inbox that responds in writing, quickly and specifically, is telling you how it will behave when something goes wrong. Vague replies, phone-call deflections, or a week of silence are also answers. Use them. If you are still building a shortlist, our review of the [best fractional real estate platforms](/blog/best-fractional-real-estate-platforms) applies this same lens across the market.

Here is the full list. The four sections that follow explain each theme.

1. **Which legal entity will I actually own a stake in,** and how is it related to the platform company?
2. **Which securities framework covers this offering:** Regulation A, Regulation D, or something else?
3. **Where can I read your filings on EDGAR,** including the most recent annual report?
4. **Who holds investor funds before the offering closes,** and is that party a third-party escrow agent?
5. **Who maintains the official record of my ownership:** your database, or an independent transfer agent?
6. **What are all three fee layers,** upfront, ongoing, and at exit, applied to a sample investment?
7. **Are the numbers on your marketing pages gross or net of all fees,** and can you show the same deal both ways?
8. **How many deals have you taken full cycle,** and what did investors receive at exit, net of fees?
9. **If your company shuts down, who services my investment,** and where do the ownership records go?
10. **What are the exact redemption terms:** windows, discounts, caps, and your right to refuse or suspend?

## What should you ask about filings and structure?

Ask three things: which legal entity issues your shares, which securities framework the offering uses, and where the filings live on EDGAR. Most fractional platforms sell securities under Regulation A or Regulation D, and the difference decides who can invest, what must be disclosed, and how much public paper trail exists. A good written answer names the exact issuer and links the filing, not the homepage.

The framework matters because it sets the disclosure floor. A [Regulation A](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/regulation-a) Tier 2 issuer can raise up to $75 million per 12 months, may accept non-accredited investors capped at 10% of the greater of their annual income or net worth per offering, and must file a Form 1-A offering circular plus a public annual Form 1-K. A Regulation D Rule 506(c) issuer faces no offering cap but must limit itself to verified accredited investors, while Rule 506(b) permits a limited number of sophisticated non-accredited investors and bars general solicitation. Our side-by-side guide to [Reg A versus Reg D for fractional investors](/blog/reg-a-vs-reg-d-for-fractional-investors) covers the tradeoffs in depth.

Then verify. Paste the issuer's exact name into [EDGAR full-text search](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/) and read what comes back. The check is quick. A good written answer names the issuing entity, states the framework, and links directly to its filing index. A platform that claims a Regulation A offering but shows nothing on EDGAR has just answered a question you did not even ask.

## Who holds your money and your shares?

Two custody questions decide how safe the plumbing is: who holds your funds before the offering closes, and who maintains the official record of your ownership afterward. The good answer names a third-party escrow agent for the first and an independent transfer agent for the second. If the platform itself plays both roles, your money and your proof of ownership depend on one company's database.

Before closing, your funds should sit with a third-party [escrow](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/escrow) agent, not in the platform's operating account. Escrow means an independent party holds the money until the offering's conditions are met, and returns it if they are not. Ask for the escrow agent's name. A good answer states it in one sentence and matches what the offering circular says.

After closing, ask who keeps the official register of ownership. An independent transfer agent maintains that record outside the platform's own systems, which is what lets your position survive a platform failure. And if a broker-dealer participates in the offering, run its name through [BrokerCheck](https://brokercheck.finra.org/) before you fund anything. It is free, and it is fast.

## What do the fees add up to over a full hold?

Fractional deals typically carry three fee layers: an upfront charge when the deal is assembled, an annual management fee while you hold, and a fee or profit share at exit. Ask for all three in writing, applied to a sample investment, on one page. Then ask the question platforms like least: are the advertised numbers shown gross or net of those fees?

Small percentages get large over long holds. A 1% annual management fee across a 10-year hold consumes roughly a tenth of your starting capital, and that is before any upfront or exit fees are counted. Typical platform-reported annual yields run 4 to 9%, which makes the gross-versus-net question decisive: the same deal can look meaningfully different depending on which side of the fees the marketing page sits on. A good answer shows both versions without being pushed.

One more layer hides behind the fees: taxes. Most fractional deals are structured as pass-through LLCs that issue a [Schedule K-1](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1065sk1), not a 1099, which changes your filing timeline and your after-tax outcome. Our guide to [how fractional real estate is taxed](/blog/how-fractional-real-estate-is-taxed) walks through the details. The fee question itself is simple. Ask for every charge, upfront, annual, and at exit, on one sample investment, in writing.

## What should you ask about the endgame?

Three questions cover the endgame: how many deals the platform has taken full cycle and what investors received, who services your investment if the platform disappears, and what the redemption terms actually say. Typical holds run 5 to 10 years, there is no maturity date, and early exits depend entirely on what is written in the offering circular, not on the marketing FAQ.

Exit history first. Ask how many deals have gone full cycle and what investors received at the end, net of fees. A young platform may honestly answer zero. That honesty is useful data; a dodge is worse. Second, ask who services the property and the investor records if the platform itself shuts down. The good answer names a backup servicer or successor arrangement and confirms the ownership register lives with the transfer agent, not on the platform's servers.

Third, redemption terms. Some platforms offer early-exit windows, often at a discount, often capped, and almost always at the platform's discretion. Unlike a bond, which carries a contractual coupon and a defined maturity date, a fractional real estate position has variable distributions and no date on which your principal comes back. Read the redemption mechanics in the offering circular. The FAQ page binds no one. The circular does.

---

## What does this checklist not solve?

Good answers make a platform trustworthy. They do not make a deal good. These ten questions filter out structural risk: hidden fees, weak custody, unverifiable claims. They say nothing about whether a specific property is well bought, well managed, or well timed, and no set of questions changes the basic facts that fractional real estate is illiquid, distributions are variable, and no return is promised.

Hannah's email will not tell her whether the building she is eyeing will perform. It will tell her whether the company standing between her and that building deserves to hold her money for the next decade. That is a different question, and it is the one most investors forget to ask.

## FAQ

**Q: Do I need to be an accredited investor to use a fractional platform?**
A: Not always. Regulation A Tier 2 offerings are open to non-accredited investors, capped at 10% of the greater of annual income or net worth per offering. Regulation D Rule 506(c) offerings are restricted to accredited investors, generally meaning $1 million in net worth excluding a primary residence, or $200,000 in income ($300,000 joint).

**Q: How can I verify a platform's answers myself?**
A: Search the issuer's name on EDGAR to read its Form 1-A offering circular and its annual Form 1-K if the offering runs under Regulation A. Check any participating broker-dealer through FINRA's BrokerCheck. When a written answer conflicts with the offering circular, the circular wins.

**Q: What does a transfer agent actually do for me?**
A: A transfer agent maintains the official register of who owns which securities, independent of the platform's own database. If the platform fails or its website disappears, that register is how your ownership survives. Ask for the transfer agent's name in writing before you invest.

**Q: What happens to my investment if the platform shuts down?**
A: Your shares sit in the issuing entity, not in the platform's brand, so they do not vanish with the website. The practical question is who takes over servicing the property and the investor records. A credible platform names a backup servicer or successor arrangement in writing.

**Q: Are fractional real estate investments insured like a bank account?**
A: No. FDIC insurance covers bank deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category, and it does not extend to securities. Fractional distributions are variable, have no maturity date, and are not insured by any government agency.
