# The Fractional Real Estate 401k Rollover: What Works and What Breaks

**Dek:** Moving an old 401(k) into fractional real estate is legal, structured, and full of tripwires. Here is the honest map: the self-directed IRA route, the two tax traps, and the people who should not bother.

[VISUAL-HERO-01]

---

## Quick Answer (60 seconds)

You cannot hold fractional real estate inside a typical employer 401(k). The workable path is a direct rollover from the old plan into a self-directed IRA at a custodian that accepts alternative assets, then investing through that account. The move preserves tax deferral, but it adds custodial fees, annual valuation demands, and two serious traps: UBIT when a property carries debt, and prohibited transaction rules that can cost the account its tax status. It works for a minority of investors with meaningful balances and long horizons. Most people should leave retirement money in plain funds and buy fractional real estate with taxable dollars instead.

**Stat cards:**
- **$23,500** - 2025 employee deferral limit for a 401(k)
- **$7,000** - 2025 IRA contribution limit
- **5 to 10 years** - typical hold period for fractional real estate deals
- **$100** - PSFnetwork minimum outside retirement accounts

*Fractional interests held in an IRA are illiquid, are not insured by any government agency, and no return is promised.*

## Opening

Jason is 52, a project manager in Atlanta, and the 401(k) from his last employer has been sitting untouched for two years. Every few months he opens the statement, considers moving the balance somewhere more interesting than a target-date fund, and closes the laptop. Lately the idea occupying his commute is real estate: specifically, whether that old account could hold fractional property stakes instead of another index fund he never thinks about.

The short answer is yes, it can be done. The longer answer is that the route runs through a self-directed IRA, a custodian who charges for everything, and a section of the tax code that punishes improvisation. This guide walks the mechanics in order, names the three tripwires that catch most first-timers, and ends with a screen for deciding whether the whole exercise is worth it for you. Often it is not.

## How do you actually move an old 401(k) into fractional real estate?

Through three moves. You open a self-directed IRA at a custodian that accepts alternative assets, request a direct rollover from the old 401(k) so the money moves institution to institution, and then direct the custodian to buy the fractional interests in the account's name. The rollover itself is a routine, tax-deferred transfer. The self-directed part is where cost, paperwork, and real risk enter.

A standard brokerage IRA will not work here. Mainstream custodians hold stocks, bonds, and funds; they do not hold private placement interests in a property LLC. You need a specialty custodian that handles alternative assets, and you need to confirm the specific platform you want will accept IRA money at all, because some do not. If the underlying product is new to you, start with our [guide to fractional real estate investing](/blog/fractional-real-estate-investing) before touching retirement funds.

1. **Open the self-directed IRA first.** Choose a custodian that explicitly supports private real estate interests, and get the full fee schedule in writing: setup, annual, per-transaction, and per-valuation charges.
2. **Request a direct rollover.** Ask the old plan administrator to send the balance straight to the new custodian. The IRS explains the mechanics on its page covering [rollovers of retirement plan and IRA distributions](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/rollovers-of-retirement-plan-and-ira-distributions). A direct transfer avoids the withholding and deadline problems an indirect rollover can create.
3. **Invest in the IRA's name, not yours.** The custodian signs the subscription documents and the asset is titled to the account. You never take possession of the funds, and the income never routes through your personal checking account.

## Why can your IRA owe tax on a deal that is supposed to be tax-deferred?

Because retirement accounts are tax-deferred, not tax-immune. When an IRA-held investment carries debt, the share of income attributable to that leverage can be taxed as unrelated debt-financed income, which flows into unrelated business income tax, or UBIT. Most fractional real estate deals use mortgage financing. So an IRA holding a leveraged property can owe tax inside the wrapper, on its own return, every year the debt exists.

This surprises almost everyone. The logic is that Congress did not want tax-exempt accounts using borrowed money to outcompete taxable investors, so income traced to leverage loses its shelter. The IRS lays out the framework on its [unrelated business income tax](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/unrelated-business-income-tax) page, and the mechanics run through the deal's paperwork: a pass-through LLC still issues a Schedule K-1 (Form 1065), except it goes to the IRA rather than to you personally.

Your custodian typically handles the filing, but the account pays the tax, and every filing is another fee. Before committing retirement money, ask the sponsor one blunt question: does this deal carry debt, and has counsel assessed the UDFI exposure? If the answer is vague, walk. For the fuller picture of how these interests are taxed in and out of retirement wrappers, see our [K-1 guide to how fractional real estate is taxed](/blog/how-fractional-real-estate-is-taxed).

## What makes a transaction prohibited, and how bad is the penalty?

A prohibited transaction is any deal between your IRA and a disqualified person: you, certain family members, or entities you control. The rules bar self-dealing in every direction. You cannot stay in a property your IRA owns, lend to it, borrow from it, or route fees to yourself. Get it wrong and you are not paying a fine on one transaction; you are risking the whole account.

Here is the part rollover marketing never mentions. The pitch sells control: your money, your assets, your choices. Inside an IRA, control is exactly the thing the rules restrict. The prohibited transaction list exists because the IRS assumes you will try to help yourself, and the penalty for guessing wrong can be the entire account's tax status. That is the trap.

Passive fractional stakes are safer ground than the classic self-directed disaster, the investor who buys a rental through an IRA and then fixes the roof personally. A minority interest in a property you have never visited leaves little room for self-dealing. But the analysis does not disappear: if you, your family, or a business you control has any relationship with the sponsor or the property, get a professional opinion before the custodian wires a dollar.

## What will a self-directed custodian require before and after you invest?

Expect paperwork on both ends. Before funding, custodians vet the asset: subscription documents, an offering circular, and confirmation the interest can be titled to the IRA. After funding, they require an updated fair market value for the position, usually every year, because the account must report what it is worth. Illiquid assets make that valuation slow, and every custodial task carries a fee.

Those fees stack on top of the platform's own economics. A 1% annual management fee across a 10-year hold already consumes roughly a tenth of your starting capital before upfront and exit fees, and custodial charges come out of the same account. Typical holds run 5 to 10 years with no early exit you can count on, which matters twice over in retirement money: once for emergencies, and again later in life when required distributions eventually demand either cash or a distributable, valued asset. Platforms differ widely on IRA support, fees, and reporting, which is exactly the comparison work covered in our guide to [choosing a fractional real estate platform](/blog/how-to-choose-fractional-real-estate-platform).

## Who should skip the rollover and keep things simple?

Most people, honestly. If the old 401(k) is your primary retirement asset, if you might need the money within a decade, or if you cannot explain UBIT and disqualified persons in your own words, the self-directed route is a poor fit. Fees compound the problem, because flat custodial charges punish small balances hardest. A boring rollover into an ordinary IRA full of index funds is a perfectly good outcome.

We think the self-directed IRA route is right for a minority of investors and wrong for most, and we would rather say that plainly than collect custodial-friction money from readers who should have kept things simple. The honest candidate has a substantial rolled-over balance, a separate emergency fund, existing conventional retirement holdings, and a genuine willingness to pay for tax advice. Everyone else has a cheaper path: keep the retirement money conventional and buy fractional real estate with taxable dollars, where the minimums start at $100 on PSFnetwork and the rulebook is far shorter.

Contribution limits explain why rollovers get all the attention here. For 2025 the employee deferral limit is [$23,500 for a 401(k) and $7,000 for an IRA](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-23500-for-2025-ira-limit-remains-7000), so fresh contributions rarely build an alternative-assets sleeve worth the custodial overhead. An old plan balance is usually the only pool big enough. For Jason, that is the real decision: not whether the maneuver is possible, but whether his balance, timeline, and appetite for paperwork clear the bar. On his numbers, it may not.

## What does a rollover not solve?

Nothing about the asset itself. Moving money into a different wrapper changes the tax treatment, not the investment. The fractional interest inside your IRA is the same illiquid, unlisted stake it would be in a taxable account: platform-reported yields in the 4 to 9% range are targets, not promises, distributions are variable with no maturity date, and no return is promised.

The wrapper does not add a safety net either. These interests are not insured by any government agency, and [FDIC insurance](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/) covers bank deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category, not securities. A rollover also does not diversify you by itself. One property can concentrate your retirement savings in a single building, a single market, and a single sponsor's execution. Treat the self-directed IRA as plumbing. The investment decision underneath it still has to survive on its own merits.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I buy fractional real estate inside my current employer's 401(k)?**
A: Almost never. Employer plans restrict you to a menu of funds selected by the plan sponsor, and private fractional property interests are not on that menu. The self-directed option generally opens up only after you leave the employer and roll the balance into an IRA.

**Q: Is a direct rollover to a self-directed IRA a taxable event?**
A: A properly executed direct rollover from a traditional 401(k) to a traditional self-directed IRA preserves tax deferral. The risk sits in indirect rollovers, where the check comes to you first and withholding and deadlines come into play. Read the IRS rollover guidance before you sign anything, and favor the direct route.

**Q: Will my IRA definitely owe UBIT on a fractional deal?**
A: Not definitely, but it should be your first diligence question. Exposure typically arrives through unrelated debt-financed income when the property carries a mortgage, and most deals do. Unleveraged deals generally avoid the issue; leveraged deals deserve a tax professional's read before you commit.

**Q: Who counts as a disqualified person for my IRA?**
A: Broadly: you, your spouse, certain close family members, entities they own or control, and the account's fiduciaries. Any transaction between the IRA and those parties, in either direction, endangers the account. When in doubt, assume the person is disqualified and get advice first.

**Q: What happens to the K-1 when the investment sits in an IRA?**
A: The sponsor's pass-through LLC still issues a Schedule K-1 (Form 1065), but it belongs to the IRA, not to you personally, so it does not go on your individual return. Your custodian uses it for account reporting and for any UBIT filing the account may owe. Keep copies anyway.

**Q: Is fractional real estate in an IRA insured like a bank deposit?**
A: No. FDIC insurance covers bank deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category, and it does not extend to securities or real estate interests. Distributions are variable, there is no maturity date, and no return is promised.
