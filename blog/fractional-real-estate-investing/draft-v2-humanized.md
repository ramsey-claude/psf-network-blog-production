# What Is Fractional Real Estate Investing? A Complete Guide for 2026

**Dek:** Own a piece of a rental property for as little as $20, without becoming a landlord. Here is how fractional real estate investing works, who it is for, and what to watch out for.

## Quick Answer (60 seconds)

Fractional real estate investing lets several investors co-own one rental property by buying shares of the legal entity that holds it, almost always an LLC. You get a proportional slice of the rent and of any gain when the property sells. The platform handles tenants, repairs, and paperwork. On some regulated US platforms a single share costs about $20, though a position that matters to your finances usually means buying many of them. Principal loss is possible and no return is promised.

**Stat cards:**
- **$20** - Per-share minimum on some regulated US platforms (e.g., Ark7)
- **4 to 9%** - Typical platform-reported annual dividend yield range
- **2012** - Year the JOBS Act expanded private offering rules
- **0** - Maintenance calls you will ever receive

## Opening

For most of the last century, the entry ticket to a US rental property was a down payment of roughly $20,000. Today it can be $20. That is not a rounding difference. It is a change in who is allowed to own income-producing property at all.

The trade is real, and you should see it before you like it. You give up every decision about the building. Your money sits still for years, not days. Fees accrue quietly across the whole hold. This guide covers how the structure actually works, where the risk genuinely sits, and how the per-square-foot model we use at PSFnetwork differs from the share-based platforms you will see alongside us.

## What is fractional real estate investing?

Several investors co-own one property through shares in a special purpose entity, nearly always an LLC that holds title. Each investor receives a proportional share of rental income and appreciation. Online platforms standardized this in the US after the JOBS Act of 2012 opened private offerings to non-accredited investors, and minimums now start as low as $20.

None of this is new. Wealthy families have split vacation homes and office buildings through partnerships for generations. What changed is who gets in the door. Platforms standardized the legal wrapper, cut the minimum by three orders of magnitude, and used Securities and Exchange Commission exemptions to market these offerings openly. Billions of dollars now sit in the category across Fundrise, Arrived, Ark7, and PSFnetwork.

One thing to be precise about, because it decides your taxes and your exit. You are not buying a deed. You are buying a security, normally a unit of membership interest in the LLC that owns the building. Deed ownership and membership interest are different instruments with different rules, and the difference surfaces at tax time and again if the platform ever runs into trouble.

## How does fractional real estate investing work?

Four steps, and they are the same almost everywhere. A platform sources a property, wraps it in an LLC, divides the LLC into shares, and sells those shares through an SEC-qualified offering. After funding, the platform runs the property and pays out net income monthly or quarterly. On sale, proceeds follow ownership percentages.

**Step 1: Property sourcing.** The platform finds a property, runs diligence, and prices it. Some specialize in single-family rentals, some in small multifamily, a few in commercial.

**Step 2: Securitization.** The property goes into an LLC, and the LLC issues membership units. The offering is filed with the SEC under one of two frameworks. Regulation A can be marketed publicly and accepts non-accredited investors, capped at $75 million per 12 months under Tier 2. Regulation D is typically limited to accredited investors.

**Step 3: Funding.** Investors buy in. Minimums range from $20 on some platforms to $5,000 on others. Most require you to be 18 with a US bank account.

**Step 4: Distributions.** The platform collects rent, pays expenses, and distributes what is left. For US investors that income usually arrives on a Schedule K-1 (Form 1065), because the LLC is a pass-through entity. A K-1 is more involved than a 1099 and often shows up late enough to delay your filing. Talk to a tax advisor before you invest, not in April.

No step carries a promise. Property values fall. Tenants default. Platforms revise their fee schedules.

## How does fractional ownership compare to owning a whole property?

Fractional lowers the entry price and removes the operational work. You pay for that with control, liquidity, and fees. Whole ownership gives you every decision and every dollar of upside, and asks for a large down payment, real ongoing labor, and all your risk parked in one building.

| Dimension | Fractional | Whole property |
|-----------|------------|----------------|
| Minimum capital | $20 to $5,000 | $20,000+ down payment typical |
| Control | None over property decisions | Full |
| Effort | Passive | Active or hire a manager |
| Liquidity | Limited, platform-dependent | Sell the property to exit |
| Tax treatment | K-1 from LLC, pass-through | Direct ownership, depreciation deductions |
| Diversification | Easy across many properties | Hard, capital-intensive |
| Risk concentration | Spread across properties | Concentrated in one asset |

Fractional then splits again by how the platform expresses your stake. Most use a per-share model where a share is a fixed dollar amount. We use a per-square-foot model, where your unit of ownership maps to actual floor area. Both are securities under the same SEC frameworks. What differs is the presentation, not the law underneath.

## What is the square-foot ownership model?

Your stake is stated in square feet instead of shares. Legally you still hold an LLC membership interest. The unit just points at something physical. Take a 1,200 square foot property priced at $400 per square foot: 50 square feet costs $20,000 and carries 4.17% of the property and its income.

Those numbers are illustrative, property pricing varies, and the instrument is still an LLC membership interest priced per square foot. The model does not change securities treatment and does not soften a single risk disclosure.

Here is why we built it this way anyway. The industry talks in shares, and a share is an abstraction with no natural size. Ask someone what 0.0417 of an LLC interest means and watch the pause. Ask what 50 square feet is and they picture a room. We think that gap is the single biggest reason first-time investors stall, and we would rather lose the sophistication points than keep the abstraction.

We are not the right platform for everyone. If you want the highest available yield, or you already think fluently in share counts and cap rates, the framing buys you nothing. It is built for the investor whose real obstacle is not risk tolerance but comprehension.

> "The clearest way to understand fractional ownership is to anchor it to something physical. A square foot is something you can stand in. A share is an abstraction. Both are securities, both carry the same risks, but one is easier to reason about." - Daniel Cho, CFA

## Is fractional real estate a good investment?

It can earn a place in a diversified portfolio if you want real estate exposure, accept illiquidity, and think in years. It is not an emergency fund, not a retirement account, and not a place for money you might need. Platform-reported annual dividend yields have run 4 to 9%, with appreciation as a possible addition. Those are reported figures, not promises, and the past does not forecast the future.

The case for it is straightforward. Lower minimums than direct ownership, income without tenant calls, exposure to something that does not move with the S&P, and a manager who handles the building.

The case against is equally plain. Liquidity is thin, because most platforms have no real secondary market. The operator can fail. Fees compound: management, sourcing, and carry all take their cut across a multi-year hold. And your outcomes depend on the platform's underwriting, which you cannot audit.

Now the part the category avoids. You will find articles comparing fractional returns to REIT returns to direct-rental ROI as though those are three numbers on the same scale. They are not. Fee treatment differs, vintages differ, and the surveys behind direct-rental figures suffer badly from survivorship bias. Anyone publishing a clean head-to-head ranking is either not reading their own footnotes or is selling something. Work in ranges, read the assumptions, and treat every historical figure as a description of the past.

Fractional real estate suits an investor who:
- Has already covered emergency savings and high-interest debt
- Can leave the capital invested for 5 or more years
- Wants real estate exposure without becoming a landlord
- Understands and accepts that principal loss is possible

It does not suit an investor who needs the capital back within 12 months, expects a promised return (no investment offers one), or is uncomfortable holding LLC membership interests.

## How do you choose a fractional real estate platform?

Five criteria, in this order: regulatory status, fees, liquidity terms, property mix, and distribution frequency. Regulatory status leads because it decides what you are told and what protections you have. Fees come next because they compound, and a single percentage point compounds into real money across a ten-year hold.

**1. Regulatory status.** Confirm the platform files under SEC Regulation A, which is open to non-accredited investors with disclosure, or Regulation D, which is typically accredited only. Verify the issuing entity yourself in SEC EDGAR. Under Regulation A Tier 2, non-accredited investors are capped at 10% of the greater of annual income or net worth per offering. Rule 506(b) allows up to 35 non-accredited but sophisticated investors alongside unlimited accredited investors, with no general solicitation. Rule 506(c) permits solicitation but accredited investors only. A platform with no US filings is not a platform you need.

**2. Fee structure.** Three layers to find: an upfront sourcing or acquisition fee, an annual management fee often between 1 and 2%, and carried interest on appreciation. Add them up before you commit, not after.

**3. Liquidity terms.** Read the operating agreement. Some platforms run a secondary market. Others hold you until the property sells, five to ten years out. Redemption windows exist on some and not on others, and the marketing page rarely says which.

**4. Property mix.** Single-family is the simplest to understand. Multifamily tends toward steadier income. Commercial carries the widest variance in both directions.

**5. Distribution frequency.** Monthly feels better than quarterly. It does not change your total return by a cent.

A defensible process looks like this: pull the SEC filing for the specific offering, read the risk factors all the way through, compare fee schedules across at least three platforms, and look for a track record that spans a full property cycle rather than a favorable vintage.

## FAQ

**Q: Is fractional real estate investing safe?**
A: It is not risk-free. Your principal can decline if property values fall, you may be unable to exit when you want, and the operator can fail. Regulated platforms must disclose these risks in their offering documents. Past performance is not indicative of future results, and no return is promised.

**Q: How is income from fractional real estate taxed?**
A: For US investors, income generally arrives on a Schedule K-1 (Form 1065) because most platforms use pass-through LLC structures. Depreciation may also pass through, which can offset part of the distribution income. Treatment varies by structure and jurisdiction. Consult a tax advisor before investing.

**Q: What happens if the platform shuts down?**
A: The property sits in an LLC separate from the platform, so a shutdown does not automatically erase the underlying value. Finding a replacement servicer takes time, though, and disputes can run months or years. Full recovery is not assured. Read the operating agreement's backup-servicer language before investing, not after.

**Q: How is fractional real estate different from a REIT?**
A: A REIT holds a portfolio of properties and, by tax rule, distributes at least 90% of taxable income to shareholders. A fractional investment is usually a stake in one specific property held by an LLC. Publicly traded REITs trade daily and spread you across a sector. Fractional lets you pick the building and gives up the liquidity.

**Q: What is the minimum investment for fractional real estate?**
A: It varies by platform. Some regulated US platforms start as low as $20. Others begin at $100 or $5,000. Regulation D offerings typically require accredited investor status, which carries its own income and net worth thresholds.

**Q: Are fractional real estate investments FDIC insured?**
A: No. FDIC insurance covers deposit accounts at insured banks, up to $250,000 per depositor per bank per ownership category. Fractional real estate investments are securities, specifically LLC membership interests, not deposits. No government agency insures them.
